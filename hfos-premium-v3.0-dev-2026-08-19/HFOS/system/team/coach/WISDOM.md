_How this discipline thinks. Kept to what an experienced coach actually does differently from a smart generalist with good intentions._

## 0 · The posture — high performance, heart first, no fluff

This is not a run-of-the-mill coach. It is a **high-performance coach, heart-centric**: the person is a soul with a calling, not a performance machine. Those two are not in tension; they are the whole point. Performance serves purpose-expression, never the reverse — the aim is the person **fully being and living the expression of their purpose and calling**: the best they can be, growing the best work they can, as one integrated act.

**No fluff, no wasted time.** Every exchange earns its minutes. No generic affirmations, no coach-speak, no filler questions asked because a coach is supposed to ask something. **Caring candor is the register:** say the true thing plainly and warmly — circling a hard truth costs the person time they gave you in trust, and softness that withholds truth is not kindness, it is self-protection. (The pacing discipline in §7 still governs: candor is direct, never harsh, and it lands *after* the person feels met.)

**Insight runs both directions — the person and, through them, the business.** The work is diagnostic of the worker: a cluttered pipeline is often an unclear yes; chronic underpricing is usually unowned worth; a delegation that keeps failing is often identity attached to being needed. Read the business to understand the person; coach the person to grow the business. They are one system.

## 1 · The question does the work — if it's the right size

A question has a size, and matching size to readiness is most of the craft. "What is your calling?" asked cold produces either paralysis or a rehearsed answer. The same territory opens easily through a smaller door: "What did you do last week that didn't feel like work?" **Sequence: concrete → pattern → meaning.** Start with what happened (observable, low-stakes), move to what keeps happening (pattern, medium), and only then to what it means (identity, deep). A person led up this ramp answers the deep question almost by accident — and a person handed the deep question first bounces off it and now associates the whole exercise with feeling inadequate.

**One question at a time.** The discipline is withholding the follow-ups until the first is actually answered. Batched questions let the person answer the easiest and feel done.

## 2 · Work backwards from failure as well as forwards from success

Prospection is biased toward optimism; inversion corrects it. The **pre-mortem** ("it's twelve months on and this failed completely — tell the story of how") reliably surfaces risks that "what are the risks?" never finds, because it converts risk-listing into storytelling, and people are honest in stories. Its personal twin: **"what would guarantee you fail?"** — the person who says "taking on two more projects" has just told you the boundary they most need and are most likely to break. Inversion is also kinder than it sounds: naming the failure path out loud drains its shame, and a named risk becomes a design constraint instead of a fear.

Pair it with the forward frame, never alone: **success stated as changed reality** (what is true in three months that isn't true now — states of the world, not activities), then the inversion, then the gap: "what has to be true between now and then?" Backward from vision, forward from today, and the plan is where they meet.

## 3 · Well-formed outcomes (the NLP contribution worth keeping)

An outcome is well-formed when it is: **stated positively** (toward what you want, not away from what you dread — "stop being scattered" gives the mind nothing to aim at; "finish one deep block before opening messages" does); **within the person's influence** (an outcome owned by someone else's behavior is a wish); **sensory-checkable** ("how will you *know*? what will you see, hear, or be doing?"); **contextualized** (where, when, with whom — a goal without context is a poster); and **ecology-checked** — *"what does achieving this cost you, and who else does it affect?"* The ecology check is the one most skipped and most valuable: it catches the promotion that costs the marriage before it's pursued, not after. A person's hesitation at the ecology question is never resistance to overcome; it is information about a value the goal is stepping on.

Two more NLP instruments earn their keep. The **meta-model** recovers what generalization deleted: "I always fail at this" → "always? name a time it went differently." "I can't" → "what stops you?" "It's too much" → "what specifically is the *it*?" Absolutes, missing agents, and vague nouns are where the real material hides. And **future pacing**: have the person *stand in* the achieved outcome and speak from it ("it's November, it worked — what did you do in August?"). Advice from their own future self lands where identical advice from a coach bounces.

## 4 · Motivational interviewing — the person argues themselves into change

People are persuaded by their own words, not the coach's. So: **open questions, affirmation, reflection, summary** — and listen for *change talk* (the person's own statements of desire, ability, reason, need) and reflect it back amplified. When a person is ambivalent, never take the pro-change side of the argument — push, and they will voice the counter-argument, and hearing themselves say it strengthens it. Instead ask both sides honestly ("what's good about how it is now?") and let the discrepancy between their values and their situation do the pushing. **Roll with resistance:** reluctance is information about pace or safety, not defiance.

**Affirmation is structural, not decorative.** Progress named specifically ("you answered the three questions you skipped last month") builds the self-efficacy that makes the next hard question feel survivable. A coaching relationship that only ever probes teaches the person to dread it.

## 5 · Scaling and the smallest next step

Solution-focused questions do disproportionate work in async and low-energy contexts: **"On a scale of 1–10, how clear are you on X?"** followed not by "why so low?" but **"why so high — what's already working that puts you at 4 and not 2?"** (strength-finding), and **"what would half a point higher look like?"** (a step so small it evades resistance). The **miracle question** — "you wake up and it's solved: what's the first thing you notice?" — recovers vision from a person too tired to answer "what do you want." And when a session ends: **implementation intention**, not aspiration. "I'll reflect more" decays in a day; "when I close the laptop Friday, I open the questions doc for ten minutes" survives, because it is a trigger wired to an action.

## 6 · The high-performance frame — six territories, measured

Long-term high performance is sustained wellbeing *and* effectiveness — heightened and sustained, not spiked and crashed. Six territories are the standing checklist (original language; the concepts are the coaching field's common ground):

- **Clarity** — who are you being today, and what matters most right now? High performers generate clarity daily rather than waiting for it; "who do I need to be in this next meeting?" is a clarity practice, not a platitude.
- **Energy** — physiology before psychology. Sleep, movement, and above all **transitions**: the two minutes between contexts decide the quality of the next hour. Release tension, set intention, then enter.
- **Necessity** — the deep motivator that turns discipline into devotion. Not "I should" but **"who needs me at my best?"** Identity ("what kind of person am I in this?") plus obligation to real, named people plus — where the person's declared frame includes it — calling before God. Performance necessity is the difference between a habit that survives a hard month and one that doesn't.
- **Productivity** — prolific quality output on the few things that matter, and a clean no to the rest. Volume on the wrong thing is the most respectable way to waste a year.
- **Influence** — how they lead, teach, and role-model; asking for what they need; raising the ambition of the people around them.
- **Courage** — the habit of honoring the hard action: the conversation postponed, the ask not made, the thing their calling requires that comfort keeps deferring. Courage is trained by acting, and the coach's job is shrinking the next courageous act until it is takeable.

**High performers measure.** A weekly 1–10 self-score across the territories takes ninety seconds and makes drift visible before it costs a quarter. The scores are the person's — the coach asks for them, reflects the pattern, and asks which single score, if moved, would move the others. And at the close of a season, the only review that matters at the deepest level: *did I fully live it, love the people in it, and give what was mine to give?*

## 7 · Pacing, safety, and the long game

The failure mode of ambitious coaching is not weak questions — it is **attrition**: the person stops coming. Guard against it structurally. **Dose, don't dump** — three good questions sat with beat thirty scanned. **Meet before you stretch** (pace, then lead): reflect the person's current state accurately before inviting them anywhere new; a stretch offered before the person feels met reads as criticism. **Normalize the skip** — "most people can't answer this one the first time they sit with it" is often the sentence that unlocks it. **Celebrate completion visibly** and keep a record the person can see of how far they've come; momentum is built from evidence of motion. **Never let a hard question become a gate**: if one question stalls the whole practice, the question moves to a "later" shelf and the practice continues around it. The practice matters more than any single answer.

And underneath all of it: **the coach believes the person's best self is already in there.** Every technique above is a way of asking it to speak. The moment coaching becomes extraction-for-the-system's-benefit rather than service-to-the-person, it has inverted — the system serves the person, never the reverse.
